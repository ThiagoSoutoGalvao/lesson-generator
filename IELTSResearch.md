# IELTS Research Report

*Prepared for Lesson Generator — evaluating what IELTS-format practice content can be legally/ethically built as a new "practice mode," following the same architecture as the existing DET Practice Mode (local hand-authored JSON, no live scraping, no reproduction of copyrighted test material, teacher-run live in Zoom).*

---

## 1. Overview

**IELTS (International English Language Testing System)** is a standardized English proficiency test for non-native speakers, used for study, work, and immigration purposes in English-speaking countries.

- **Ownership/administration:** IELTS is jointly owned and administered by three organizations collectively referred to as the "IELTS Partners":
  - **British Council**
  - **IDP IELTS** (originally IDP Education, Australia — sole owner/administrator in some markets like India)
  - **Cambridge University Press & Assessment** (formerly "Cambridge English Language Assessment") — responsible for writing test content and overseeing marking standards
  - The test has identical format and content regardless of whether a candidate books through British Council or IDP; both follow the same Cambridge-set standards.

- **Two test versions:**
  - **IELTS Academic** — for candidates applying to study at undergraduate/postgraduate level, or for professional registration (e.g. doctors, nurses) in an English-speaking environment.
  - **IELTS General Training** — for candidates migrating to English-speaking countries (Australia, Canada, UK, etc.), or for work experience/training programs below degree level, or secondary education.
  - Listening and Speaking are **identical** between the two versions. **Reading and Writing differ** (see Section 2).

- **Delivery modes:** Paper-based and computer-delivered (identical content/format, computer-delivered version is faster to get results). There is also **"IELTS for UKVI"** — the same test, but with results specifically approved for UK Visas and Immigration purposes, and **IELTS Life Skills** (a simpler A1/B1-level Speaking & Listening-only test, mainly for UK family/settlement visas) — a distinct product not covered in depth here since it doesn't share the 4-skill structure.

- **Who takes it / why:**
  - University admission (undergraduate and postgraduate) — most English-medium universities in the UK, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Ireland, and many in the US and elsewhere accept IELTS.
  - Immigration/visa applications — e.g., UK Skilled Worker visa, UK Student visa (requires **IELTS for UKVI**), Australian and Canadian permanent residency (Canada primarily uses IELTS General Training for Express Entry), New Zealand visas.
  - Professional registration — nursing/medical councils, some professional bodies.
  - Scores are valid for **2 years (24 months)** from the test date.

## 2. Test Structure

**Total test time:** ~2 hours 45 minutes for Listening + Reading + Writing (Speaking is often scheduled separately, same day or up to a week before/after).

| Section | Duration | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Listening** | ~30 minutes + 10 minutes transfer time (paper-based) | 4 parts, 40 questions total. Identical for Academic & General Training. |
| **Reading** | 60 minutes (includes answer transfer) | 3 sections, 40 questions total. **Differs between Academic and General Training.** |
| **Writing** | 60 minutes | 2 tasks. **Differs between Academic and General Training** (mainly Task 1). |
| **Speaking** | 11–14 minutes | 3 parts, face-to-face (or video) interview with a certified examiner. Identical for Academic & General Training. |

Listening, Reading, and Writing are taken back-to-back with no breaks on the same day. Speaking may be the same day or scheduled separately (within a week either side).

### Academic vs. General Training — the key differences

- **Listening & Speaking:** No difference at all between the two test versions.
- **Reading:** Both have 3 sections / 40 questions / 60 minutes, but:
  - **Academic Reading** uses 3 long passages (2,150–2,750 words total) from books, journals, magazines, newspapers, and online sources — written for a non-specialist but academically-minded audience (argumentative, descriptive, narrative styles), on topics suited to people entering university or professional registration.
  - **General Training Reading** uses shorter, more numerous, everyday-life texts split into three distinct sub-sections:
    - Section 1 — "Social Survival": notices, advertisements, timetables, everyday materials (2–3 short texts, or several very short texts).
    - Section 2 — "Workplace Survival": job descriptions, contracts, staff development/training materials (2 texts).
    - Section 3 — "General Interest": one long text from a newspaper, magazine, book, or website — more complex, similar in style to Academic passages but generally more accessible.
- **Writing:**
  - **Task 1** is the main point of divergence: Academic Task 1 asks candidates to describe/summarize visual data (a graph, chart, table, diagram, map, or process) in a short factual **report** (150 words min, ~20 min). General Training Task 1 asks candidates to write a **letter** (150 words min, ~20 min) responding to a everyday situation — formal, semi-formal, or informal register depending on who it's addressed to, always guided by 3 bullet points that must be addressed.
  - **Task 2** is the **same format in both versions**: a discursive/argumentative essay (250 words min, ~40 min) responding to a point of view, argument, or problem. Task 2 counts for roughly twice the weight of Task 1 in the overall Writing band.

## 3. Question Types in Detail

### 3.1 Listening (4 parts, 10 questions each, 40 total)

Context/topic progression across the 4 parts (this progression is fixed and is itself a "format" fact, not copyrightable content):
- **Part 1** — a conversation between two speakers in an everyday social context (e.g., booking travel, making an inquiry, filling out a form over the phone).
- **Part 2** — a monologue in an everyday social context (e.g., a speech about a local facility, an announcement, a guided tour talk).
- **Part 3** — a conversation between up to four people in an educational/training context (e.g., university students discussing an assignment, possibly with a tutor).
- **Part 4** — a monologue on an academic subject (e.g., a lecture excerpt).

Each recording is played only once (no repeats, since the January 2020 format update, which also renamed "sections" to "parts" and removed the pre-Part-1 practice example). Accents include British, Australian, New Zealand, North American, and others.

**Question types used across the 4 parts (types vary; not every type appears in every test):**
1. Multiple choice (single or multiple answer selection)
2. Matching (matching a list of items to a set of options)
3. Plan/map/diagram labelling (label a floorplan, map, or diagram from what's heard)
4. Form completion
5. Note completion
6. Table completion
7. Flow-chart completion
8. Summary completion
9. Sentence completion
10. Short-answer questions

*(Sources vary on whether this is "6 categories" or "10 distinct types" — the 10-item breakdown above is the more granular, drill-friendly list; the official page groups several of these together, e.g. "form/note/table/flow-chart/summary completion" as one family and "sentence completion" as another, alongside multiple choice, matching, and plan/map/diagram labelling.)*

### 3.2 Reading (3 sections, 40 questions total — official Academic-format page lists 11 distinct question types, which is the fullest breakdown; General Training reuses the same question-type toolkit against different text types)

1. **Multiple choice** — select one (or sometimes more) correct answer(s) from a list of options.
2. **True/False/Not Given** — for statements about **facts** in the passage: True = agrees with the text, False = contradicts the text, Not Given = no information either way.
3. **Yes/No/Not Given** — same 3-way logic as above but for statements about the **writer's opinions/claims** rather than facts (used in passages with an argumentative slant).
4. **Matching information** — match a piece of information/detail to the paragraph (labelled A, B, C…) that contains it.
5. **Matching headings** — match a list of headings (given as Roman numerals) to paragraphs/sections of the text, based on the paragraph's main idea.
6. **Matching features** — match a set of statements/descriptions to a list of options (e.g., match a claim to which of several researchers made it).
7. **Matching sentence endings** — complete a sentence beginning by matching it to the correct ending from a list.
8. **Sentence completion** — fill a gap in a sentence, usually with a word-limit instruction (e.g. "no more than two words"), taken from the passage.
9. **Summary/note/table/flow-chart completion** — fill gaps in a summary (or note-form outline, table, or flow-chart) of part of the text, either using words from the passage or selecting from a given word bank/box.
10. **Diagram label completion** — label parts of a diagram using words from the passage.
11. **Short-answer questions** — answer a question with a short factual answer taken directly from the text, under a word-limit.

### 3.3 Writing

- **Task 1 (Academic)** — report on visual data: line graph, bar chart, pie chart, table, diagram/process, or map. Candidate must summarize/describe/compare the key trends and data — no opinion required, min 150 words, ~20 minutes.
- **Task 1 (General Training)** — letter in response to a situation (personal, semi-formal, or formal register depending on recipient), always guided by 3 bullet points the candidate must cover (e.g. request information, complain, explain a situation, invite someone). Min 150 words, ~20 minutes.
- **Task 2 (both versions, same format)** — a discursive essay responding to a prompt, min 250 words, ~40 minutes. Falls into roughly five recurring prompt sub-types (useful for drill variety):
  1. Opinion (agree/disagree with a statement)
  2. Discuss both views and give your own opinion
  3. Advantages and disadvantages
  4. Problem/solution (or causes/solutions)
  5. Two-part/direct-question essay

Assessed on 4 criteria (each ~25% weight): **Task Achievement** (Task 1) / **Task Response** (Task 2), **Coherence and Cohesion**, **Lexical Resource**, **Grammatical Range and Accuracy**.

### 3.4 Speaking (3 parts, 11–14 minutes total, one-on-one with a certified examiner — either in person or via video call)

- **Part 1 — Introduction and Interview (4–5 min):** examiner asks general questions about the candidate (home, family, work/study, interests, familiar everyday topics). Warm-up, identity-verification stage.
- **Part 2 — Long Turn / "Cue Card" (3–4 min total: 1 min prep + 1–2 min speaking):** candidate is given a card with a topic and 3–4 bullet points to address, has 1 minute to prepare (with notepad), then speaks for 1–2 minutes without interruption. Examiner may ask 1 brief follow-up question after.
- **Part 3 — Two-Way Discussion (4–5 min):** examiner asks more abstract follow-up questions thematically linked to the Part 2 topic, requiring analysis, speculation, comparison, and justified opinions — a step up in abstraction/difficulty from Parts 1 and 2.

Assessed on 4 criteria (each 25% weight): **Fluency and Coherence**, **Lexical Resource**, **Grammatical Range and Accuracy**, **Pronunciation**.

## 4. Scoring

- **Scale:** 0–9 band scale, in 0.5 increments (e.g. 6.5, 7.5). Each of the 4 skills is scored independently on this same 0–9 scale.

| Band | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| 9 | Expert User |
| 8 | Very Good User |
| 7 | Good User |
| 6 | Competent User |
| 5 | Modest User |
| 4 | Limited User |
| 3 | Extremely Limited User |
| 2 | Intermittent User |
| 1 | Non-User |
| 0 | Did Not Attempt the Test |

- **Listening & Reading:** scored objectively — 1 raw mark per correct answer out of 40, no penalty for wrong answers, then converted to a band via a published (but section/version-specific) raw-score-to-band conversion table. Approximate benchmarks:
  - Listening: ~16/40 ≈ Band 5, ~23/40 ≈ Band 6, ~30/40 ≈ Band 7, ~35/40 ≈ Band 8.
  - Reading (Academic): ~15/40 ≈ Band 5, ~23/40 ≈ Band 6, ~30/40 ≈ Band 7, ~35/40 ≈ Band 8.
  - Reading (General Training) is graded on an easier curve than Academic Reading — roughly one band more raw marks are needed for the same band score (e.g. ~23/40 ≈ Band 5, ~30/40 ≈ Band 6, ~35/40 ≈ Band 7), reflecting that GT passages are considered easier.
  - These conversion tables are recalibrated per test administration, so exact cut-offs shift slightly test to test — the numbers above are illustrative, not fixed.
- **Writing & Speaking:** scored subjectively by certified examiners against the 4 published assessment criteria per skill (each criterion weighted equally, 25%), each criterion scored in whole/half bands, averaged to the section band.
- **Overall Band Score:** the arithmetic mean of the four section band scores (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking), each equally weighted, rounded to the nearest 0.5:
  - Average ending in .25 → rounds **up** to the next half band.
  - Average ending in .75 → rounds **up** to the next whole band.
  - (e.g. mean of 6.75 → reported as 7.0; mean of 6.25 → reported as 6.5; mean of 6.1 → reported as 6.0.)

- **Typical institutional/visa requirements** (illustrative, always varies by institution/program/route and changes over time — should not be treated as current authoritative guidance):
  - UK Visas and Immigration (UKVI) minimum for most student visa routes: **5.5 overall**, no band below 5.5 (requires **IELTS for UKVI**, not standard IELTS, for the visa itself — though standard IELTS may still be accepted by the university for admission).
  - UK undergraduate admission: commonly **6.0–6.5** overall.
  - UK postgraduate / competitive programs: commonly **6.5–7.0**.
  - Russell Group / top-tier UK universities: often **7.0 overall, no band below 6.5**.
  - Skilled-worker/immigration point-scoring routes (UK, Australia, Canada) often reward higher bands (e.g. Canada's Express Entry gives progressively more Comprehensive Ranking System points at higher Canadian Language Benchmark equivalents, which map to IELTS General Training bands).

## 5. What's Practice-Able Without Infringement

This section is the core deliverable for deciding what Lesson Generator can safely build, following the same lens already applied to DET Practice Mode.

### Safe to build (structural/format-based, no official content involved)

Everything below is a **test format** — a structure, a question mechanic, a skill, a timing convention. Formats and general "types of task" are not copyrightable; only the **specific texts, audio recordings, images, and question sets** that the IELTS Partners have written and published are protected. Exactly like DET Practice Mode, original hand-authored content built *in the style of* these formats is safe:

- **Reading drills** — build original passages (any topic/length matching the 3-section Academic pattern, or social/workplace/general-interest GT pattern) with hand-authored:
  - True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given item sets
  - Matching headings / matching information / matching features / matching sentence endings
  - Sentence, summary, table, flow-chart, and diagram-label completion
  - Short-answer questions
  - This maps very directly onto Lesson Generator's existing template toolkit (True/False/Not Given, Cloze/gap-fill, Matching Pairs, Sentence Transformation-style completion) — much of the mechanical UI likely already exists and only needs IELTS-flavored content and framing.
- **Listening drills** — original short scripts/dialogues (teacher-read live, or scripted for the teacher to read aloud, matching the app's "no live audio API" pattern used elsewhere) styled as the 4 IELTS contexts (social conversation, social monologue, educational conversation, academic monologue), with form/note/table/plan-labelling/matching/multiple-choice question sets — same mechanical shape as content already built for Pronunciation and DET Practice.
- **Writing practice prompts** — original Task 1 data-description prompts (invented charts/graphs/processes/maps) and Task 1 letter-situation prompts (invented scenarios + bullet points), and original Task 2 essay prompts across the five recurring sub-types (opinion, discussion, advantages/disadvantages, problem/solution, two-part). A teacher can review the student's written response live — no auto-scoring needed, mirroring DET's "no scoring engine" rule.
- **Speaking practice prompts** — original Part 1 interview-style question banks by topic, original Part 2 cue cards (topic + bullet points, teacher gives 1-minute prep time live), original Part 3 discussion follow-up question banks. This maps almost exactly onto the **DET Speaking Practice architecture already built** (`SpeakingPromptDrill.jsx`, prep/reveal pattern, no timers per the explicit "no timers anywhere" user direction on the DET side — the same design philosophy would likely transfer well here, though that's a product decision, not a legal one).
- **Band-descriptor-informed feedback framing** — a teacher can reference the *public* band-descriptor language (competent/good/very good user, etc. — these are published openly by IELTS as guidance for candidates and teachers) to give the student informal, non-binding feedback, as long as the app never claims to be issuing an official or officially-calibrated score.
- **General terminology** — words like "Task 1," "Task 2," "cue card," "band score," "Listening/Reading/Writing/Speaking," "True/False/Not Given" are descriptive/generic testing terminology, safe to use in prose/UI copy (e.g. "IELTS-style Task 2 practice") in the same descriptive, non-branding way DET Practice Mode already refers to "the DET" in its own internal docs without adopting Duolingo's branding.

### Risk areas — avoid

- **Copyrighted official content** — do not copy, adapt, retype, or closely paraphrase any actual passage, audio script, image/graph, or question set from official IELTS practice tests, past papers, Cambridge IELTS practice-test books, or the official ielts.org sample materials. Per `ielts.org`'s own copyright & trademark statement: copyright is owned by "IELTS Partners" (British Council / IELTS Australia Pty Ltd / Cambridge English), content is licensed for **personal, non-commercial use only**, republishing/modifying/redistributing it (on another site, in another product, etc.) is explicitly prohibited without written permission, and proprietary/copyright notices may not be stripped. Since Lesson Generator is a commercial (or commercial-track, per the Phase 11 monetization roadmap) product, even "personal use" official material should not be embedded into app content — this is a materially higher-risk pattern than hand-authoring originals, and stricter than the DET precedent already required.
- **Trademark/branding** — "IELTS" is a registered trademark of the IELTS Partners. Avoid:
  - Naming the feature/product in a way that could imply official IELTS affiliation, certification, or endorsement (e.g. don't call it "IELTS Official Practice" or use the IELTS logo/wordmark styling).
  - Following the precedent DET Practice Mode already set (amber owl mascot deliberately *not* Duolingo green, explicit avoidance of official branding/logos) — an analogous non-IELTS-styled visual identity for an IELTS-flavored feature would be the safe move.
  - Safer naming patterns seen in the wild and worth adopting: descriptive/comparative phrasing like "IELTS-style practice," "IELTS-format speaking drills," etc. rather than presenting the tool as IELTS-branded software. A visible disclaimer ("not affiliated with or endorsed by IELTS, British Council, IDP, or Cambridge") costs nothing and meaningfully reduces risk, mirroring DET Practice Mode's "no scoring engine that claims to predict a real score" rule.
- **No official scoring claims** — do not build or market any feature that claims to produce a real/certified/predictive IELTS band score. This mirrors the existing DET rule exactly ("no scoring engine, no recording, no DET branding/verbatim content") and is doubly important for IELTS because the band-score system feeds directly into real visa/university admission decisions — a false sense of certainty here is a bigger practical/reputational risk than with DET.
- **No recordings of copyrighted listening material** — if building Listening drills, any audio must be freshly recorded/TTS-generated from original scripts (same pattern as the Pronunciation feature's self-hosted TTS/Wikimedia audio), never ripped or adapted from official IELTS listening tracks.
- **Derivative-but-close content** — closely imitating a specific, identifiable official passage/prompt (e.g. reproducing a well-known "IELTS Cambridge Book 15 Test 3" reading passage nearly verbatim with minor word swaps) is a copyright risk even though the *question type* is safe; the practice should be "same format, wholly original content," same as DET.

### Net takeaway

The DET Practice Mode precedent transfers directly: **format/structure/skill-type = safe to build original content for; specific published texts, audio, images, and official branding = must be avoided.** IELTS carries somewhat higher stakes than DET because (a) its content is explicitly copyrighted by named commercial rights-holders with a public copyright/trademark statement prohibiting redistribution, and (b) the band score has direct, high-stakes downstream consequences (visas, degree admission) that make false score-prediction claims riskier than with DET. A disclaimer of non-affiliation and strict "hand-authored originals only, no scraped/typed-in official material" discipline (already the app's practice for every other feature) fully covers the same safe zone DET Practice Mode already operates in.
